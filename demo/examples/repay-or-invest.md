# Repay or invest?

*Pay a mortgage off, or keep the debt and invest the money that would
have repaid it? A worked model on real UK data from 1975. Change the
assumptions in @tbl:assume - the start date, the term, the kind of
mortgage - and every figure and chart follows. Not financial advice.*

[[_TOC_]]

## The question

A repayment mortgage pays the loan off as it goes: each month's
payment is interest plus a slice of the capital, and at the end of the
term nothing is owed. The alternative is to keep the debt where it is -
pay only the interest - and invest the capital part of the same payment
each month instead, hoping the investments grow faster than the
mortgage charges.[^endowment]

Both choices cost the same each month. The question is which leaves
you better off at the end of the term: no debt, or the whole loan still
owed with a pot of investments beside it to repay it from.

## The data

Three public datasets, imported with **Import data › Open data** and
kept in this document, so it opens offline and gives the same answer
next year. Each is monthly, from as far back as the source goes to the
last month all have.

- Bank Rate from the Bank of England [@boe_bank_rate], from January
  1975 - what a tracker mortgage follows (@fig:bank-chart).
- Mortgage rates from the Bank of England [@boe_rates], from January
  1995: the average 2-year and 5-year fixed rates at 75% loan-to-value,
  and the standard variable rate (@fig:rates-chart).
- UK share prices from the OECD [@oecd_uk_shares], from January 1975:
  a monthly index of the UK stock market (@fig:shares-chart, below).

So a tracker can start in any month from January 1975; fixes and the
standard variable rate from January 1995.

### What the investments earn

The investments in this model grow with @fig:shares-chart, the OECD's
index of UK share prices: one number a month for the UK stock market as
a whole, set to 100 in 2015. When the index rises 1% in a month, the
invested money rises 1% that month; when it falls, so do the
investments. On top of that, each month adds a twelfth of the
**dividends** assumption in @tbl:assume.

What the index is not:

- **Not a total return.** It follows share prices only. The dividends
  companies pay out - historically a large part of what UK shares have
  returned over decades - are not in it. That is why the model adds
  them back as an assumption you can change, rather than as measured
  data.
- **Not a fund.** Nobody can buy the index itself. A real tracker fund
  charges fees, follows a particular index (a FTSE index, say) and
  trails it a little.
- **Not adjusted for inflation.** It is in the pounds of each month,
  as the mortgage and its payments are.
- **Not taxed.** Gains and dividends are taken as untaxed - as inside
  an ISA.
- **Not within the month.** One figure per month, so moves inside a
  month - a crash and recovery in the same weeks - do not show.

'too early: Bank Rate data begins in January 1975' : 'too early: ' + @assume.r1.mortgage + ' data begins in January 1995 - a tracker can start from January 1975') : 'fine'"}
data:
  - [Assumptions, ""]
:::

- **loan** - what is borrowed.
- **start** - the month the mortgage begins: from January 1975 for a
  tracker, from January 1995 for fixes and the standard variable rate
  (that is where their data begins; @tbl:check, just above, says if
  the start is too early). A start in October 2001 with a 25-year term
  ends in September 2026, so the whole term is real history; so does a
  tracker from January 1990, through the high rates of 1990.
- **term** - when the loan must be repaid. Repaying clears it a little
  every month; holding the debt clears it in one go at the end, from
  the investments. If the term runs past the data, the model stops at
  the data's last month and says how much of the term was left
  (@tbl:period).
- **mortgage** - how the rate is set, one of:
  - `2-year fix` or `5-year fix` - the rate is fixed when the deal
    starts and kept for two or five years, then the mortgage moves to
    the fix on offer at that time, and so on;
  - `tracker` - Bank Rate plus **margin**, changing whenever Bank Rate
    does;
  - `variable` - the lender's standard variable rate.
- **margin** - a tracker's margin over Bank Rate (used only by
  trackers). Margins have ranged from under 1% before 2008 to 2% and
  more since.
- **dividends** - the dividend yield added to the share index, each
  year, reinvested.
- **monthly** - what you pay each month, the same either way. 0 means
  the normal repayment (it changes as rates do). More than that - say
  1500 - and repaying overpays: the loan clears early, and from then on
  the whole monthly amount is invested, while holding the debt invests
  whatever the interest leaves. It never drops below the normal
  repayment.

Both choices pay the same rate: the model asks what to do with the
payment, not which lender to use.

:::

Both choices pay the same each month: `monthly`, or the normal
repayment - worked out again each month for what the repayer still
owes, the rate and the time left - if that is more. What differs is
where it goes.

- **Repaying** pays interest only on what is *still owed*. In the first
  month that is the whole loan, so the interest is the same both ways;
  then the balance shrinks, the interest with it, and more and more of
  each payment repays capital (@fig:repay-split). Paying more than the
  normal repayment clears the loan early; after that the whole monthly
  amount is invested.
- **Holding the debt** pays interest on the *whole loan* every month,
  so its interest moves only with the rate - and the investment is
  what is left: `invested = payment - interest_only`. It moves the
  opposite way to the interest: when rates rise, interest takes more
  and less is invested; when rates fall, more is invested
  (@fig:hold-split). A fix shows as flat steps that change at each
  renewal.

## The model

@tbl:plan works month by month - up to 40 years of months, blank past
the term or past the data. Each column is a formula over the imported
data and the assumptions:

- `live` - 1 for a month inside the term and inside the data.
- `month` - the end of the month: `eomonth(start, n - 1)`.
- `rate` - the mortgage rate that month. A fix reads the fixed rate on
  offer when its deal began: `asof(eomonth(start, floor((n - 1) / 24) *
  24), @rates.col.date, @rates.col.fixed_2y)`; a tracker reads Bank
  Rate and adds the margin.
- `owed_repaying` - the repayment mortgage: what is still owed. Each
  month it gains the interest and loses the payment - the normal
  repayment, `pmt(rate / 100 / 12, months left, owed)`, or `monthly`
  if that is more - and stops at nothing.
- `outgoing` - what is paid that month, the same either way.
- `payment`, `interest_repaying`, `capital_repaid` - the repayer's
  mortgage payment and how it splits; `invested_repaying` is what is
  left of `outgoing` once the loan is cleared, invested.
- `interest_only` and `invested` - holding the debt: the interest on
  the whole loan, and the rest of the same payment, invested.
- `index`, `pot_repaying` and `pot` - the share index that month, and
  each choice's investments, dividends included.
- `net_repaying` and `net_investing` - where each choice leaves you:
  the investments minus what is still owed. `ahead` is how far holding
  the debt is ahead (negative: behind).

## What this leaves out

- **Leverage.** Holding the debt is investing borrowed money: the
  investments must beat the mortgage rate just to break even, and the
  whole loan is still owed at the end. If they fall short, the
  difference has to be found. That is how many endowment mortgages
  ended.[^endowment]
- **Timing.** The result depends on the start date: a crash near the
  end of the term hurts most, because there is no time left to
  recover. Try other start dates in @tbl:assume.
- **Dividends are assumed**, not measured: the share index is a price
  index. Change `dividends` to see how much the answer depends on it.
- **Interest-only is not a free choice.** Lenders offer it only with a
  credible plan to repay the capital, often at lower loan-to-values or
  higher rates; this model gives both choices the same rate.
- **The index is not a fund.** It is the OECD's UK share price index,
  a monthly figure for the whole market; a real tracker has fees and
  follows its own index.
- **Older rates.** Before 1995 the only rate is Bank Rate, so only a
  tracker can start then; real trackers in the 1970s and 1980s were
  rare (most mortgages followed lenders' variable rates), so read an
  early tracker as "a mortgage that followed Bank Rate".
- **Rates are averages.** The Bank of England series are average quoted
  rates for new mortgages at 75% loan-to-value: one household's deals,
  fees and loan-to-value would differ.[^rate]
- **Tax.** Investing inside an ISA is assumed; outside one, gains and
  dividends may be taxed.
